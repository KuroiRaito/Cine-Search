What's actually there

Four questions had to be answered before any of this could be drawn. All four were answered by calling the API, not by reading documentation.

Characters exist — as strings on a credit, not as things

GET /movie/550/credits
cast[0] keys: … name, character, credit_id, order
  Edward Norton as "Narrator" · Brad Pitt as "Tyler Durden"
  Helena Bonham Carter as "Marla Singer"

GET /tv/1396/aggregate_credits   // Breaking Bad — the TV-correct call
cast[0].roles[0] = { "credit_id": "52542282760ee313280017f9",
                     "character": "Walter White", "episode_count": 62 }

GET /person/17419/combined_credits   // Bryan Cranston
234 cast roles, 221 carry a character string
A favourite character is one string: its credit_id. GET /credit/52542282760ee313280017f9 returns the character name, the person with their profile_path, and the title with its poster_path — everything a card needs, from one stored id. No triple to keep in sync, no free-text tag to normalise. This is better than the workaround, and it is TMDB's own identifier for exactly “this person, playing this character, in this title”.

But there is no character entity, and that changes the interface

The anime case, checked because you asked for Levi

GET /tv/1429/aggregate_credits   // Attack on Titan, 221 cast
  Hiroshi Kamiya as "Levi (voice)"   profile_path: /5aAAOlQ2hW…jpg

…same call at language=ja-JP:

Two things break for animation. The character name carries a (voice) suffix that has to be stripped for display. And the face attached to that credit is Hiroshi Kamiya's, not Levi's — for live action the actor is the character's face, and for animation it is a photograph of a stranger. §04 handles both.

Your profile fills itself in.
Mark something a favourite and it appears here. Nothing to set up.

The profile is tabbed, not one long scroll. This is the structural change the benchmark forced. AL-chan runs Overview · Anime List · Manga List · Favorites · Stats · Social · Reviews, and with five favourite shelves, nine rankable stat dimensions and two distribution bars, one column of everything is a page nobody reaches the bottom of. Four tabs here: Overview (identity, headline stats, a shelf preview), Favourites (all five shelves in full), Stats (the dimensions and the bars), Library (what the library module already draws). The banner, name and handle persist above the tabs on every one.
The order is not arbitrary. Identity, then evidence, then taste. The stat strip sits directly under the bio because it is the cheapest possible proof that the rest of the page is worth reading — and on a new account it is the honest zero rather than a hidden section.

03

Five shelves

AL-chan carries five: anime, manga, characters, staff and studios. The film-and-TV translation is not one-to-one — manga has no counterpart, so films and series take the two title shelves — but studios translate directly, and that shelf was missing from the first draft of this page.

ShelfCardStored asCapSource
FilmsPoster 2:3, title, yeartmdb_id + 'movie'8user_library.is_favourite — already exists
SeriesPoster 2:3, title, yeartmdb_id + 'tv'8Same column, different media_type
PeopleRound face, name, the role you know them forperson_id8New. Actors and crew share one shelf.
CharactersSquare face, character, title, actorcredit_id8New. §04.
StudiosWordmark on a plate, title countcompany_id8Added after the benchmark. AL-chan has a studios shelf and it is the one shelf I had missed — A24, Ghibli and Blumhouse are as much a reason to watch something as a director. TMDB production_companies.
Films and series are separate shelves; actors and directors are not. The split that matters in this product is the one that already runs through it — a film is finished, a series is in progress, and they carry different status vocabularies. A cinematographer and a lead actor are both just people whose work you follow, and forcing them apart would mean deciding what to do with someone who is both.
Eight, and the cap is the feature. A favourites shelf with no limit becomes a second watchlist, and then it says nothing. Eight is two full rows on a phone rail and forces a choice. When the ninth is added, the sheet asks which one it replaces — it does not silently refuse, and it does not silently drop the oldest.

Ordering

Favourites are ordered by the person, not by rating or recency — the first film on the shelf is a statement. Drag to reorder on touch; on a pointer, the same handle plus ↑/↓ keyboard support. Order is stored as an integer; a gap-based sequence (10, 20, 30) so a reorder is one row's update rather than eight.

04

Characters

The shelf you asked for, and the one with real problems behind it. All of them are solvable; none of them are guessable.

The card

LineShowsTypeWhy there
Face96×96, --radius-12—Square, not round: a round face is a person in this system, and a character is not a person.
OverlayActor's name, bottom of the face--f-m 9px on a scrimThe face is the actor's, so the face should say so. Removes the lie rather than hiding it.
PrimaryCharacter name--text-caption 600The thing being favourited.
SecondaryTitle--f-m 9px“Levi” is meaningless without it, and two shows can share a character name.

Three rules the API forces

R-C1 — strip the role qualifier. TMDB stores "Levi (voice)", "Walter White (uncredited)", "Young Paul (archive footage)". Display strips a trailing parenthetical; the raw string is never shown. The stored value is the credit_id, so nothing is lost by cleaning the display.
R-C2 — animation falls back to the poster. For live action the actor's photograph is the character's face and the card is honest. For animation it is a photograph of the voice actor, which is worse than no face at all. Detect via the title's genre 16 (Animation) and use the poster, cropped square, with the character name over it. The actor line then reads Voice · Hiroshi Kamiya.
R-C3 — a character is a credit, so it can vanish. TMDB credits are user-edited and credit_ids can be removed when a title's cast is restructured. A card whose /credit/{id} 404s keeps the character name and title from our own stored copy and drops to a poster face — it never disappears from someone's profile because an editor tidied a cast list. Store the display strings alongside the id for exactly this reason.
The denormalisation is deliberate and it is the second time this project has made this call. catalog_people exists so Villeneuve's biography is not repeated inside 26 titles. Here the opposite applies: the character name and title are copied onto the favourite row, because the alternative is a profile that degrades when someone else edits TMDB. Normalise what you own; copy what you do not.

Ordering the cast by episode count is what makes this usable. Breaking Bad returns 221 cast members and Attack on Titan returns 221 as well. TMDB's default order is billing order, which buries a recurring character behind one-scene guests. aggregate_credits gives total_episode_count per person — sort by it and the character somebody actually means is in the first five. For films there is no episode count, so billing order is correct and the list is short anyway.
A search box inside step two, not on step one. Once a title is chosen, filtering 221 names by typing is useful and it works — because at that point the cast list is in hand. What cannot exist is a character search across the whole catalogue.

Banner and avatar

There are two ways to put an image on a profile, and they produce genuinely different products — not just different build costs.

Two ways to get an image, and the cheap one is also the better one

Pick from the catalogueUpload your own
StoredA backdrop_path string — 40 bytesA file, in a bucket that must first exist
To choose oneTwo taps, from titles you already watchedLeave the app, find a file, crop it
Always looks rightYes — every backdrop is 16:9 and made to sit behind somethingOnly after cropping, and a busy photo fights the name on top of it
To buildNothing newStorage that does not exist yet
Feels like“My banner is the desert from Dune”“My banner is a photo”
Recommendation: ship “pick from the catalogue” first, and it may be all you ever need. Every title already carries a 16:9 backdrop — a still from something you actually watched, which on a film tracker is more personal than an uploaded photograph, not less. It also composes correctly by construction: a backdrop is already an image designed to have things sit on top of it. Upload stays fully drawn (E4, E5), so adding it later is a build rather than a redesign.

Banner source, in order of offer

SourceShown asNote
From your favouritesBackdrops of the 8 films and 8 series on your shelvesFirst, because they are already the titles you chose to display.
From your libraryBackdrops of everything watched, newest firstSearchable. A title with no backdrop is not offered.
A colourSix gradients from the scalesThe honest default. Never a stock photograph.
UploadIf the bucket existsJPEG/PNG/WebP, ≤ 4 MB, cropped client-side to 4:1 before upload.
The banner is furniture, not content. It sits behind a scrim that fades to --bg by 62% of its height, in both themes, because the name and handle sit on it. Artwork has no theme — the scrim is the same recipe the cover uses, and nothing readable is ever placed on the unscrimmed part.
The avatar is an initial until it is not. No default illustration, no generated identicon: the first letter of the display name on a two-stop gradient keyed to the account id. It is legible at 64px, needs no request, and never looks like a stock silhouette. An uploaded avatar is a square crop, ≤ 1 MB, rendered at --radius-full.
07

Stats

This section was four tiles. Then I read what AL-chan actually computes for one person, and four tiles is not a profile — it is a summary of a profile.

The thing I had missed: AL-chan carries a mean score on every dimension, not just overall. Pulled from a real account with 1,497 titles — Action = 724 titles, mean 54.9 against Comedy = 703, mean 59.2. Nearly the same volume, four points apart. You watch more action and you enjoy comedy more, and that sentence is only available because the mean rides along with the count. A bare count says what you consumed. A count with a mean says something about you.

Films and series get their own block

AL-chan splits anime from manga — two headline rows, each with its own totals, mean and distribution bar. The translation here is films and series, and it is not cosmetic: a film has a runtime and no episodes, a series has both, and one blended “titles” number hides which you actually are.

The distribution bar

Under each block, one stacked bar — the signature element of an AL-chan profile, and the one thing that makes the numbers feel like a life rather than a receipt. The real account reads:

COMPLETED 1189   PLANNING 840   CURRENT 204   DROPPED 95   PAUSED 9
This lands here for free. Projector already has six watch states, each with its own hue and its own icon — the palette was built in the foundations and has had nowhere to prove itself. The bar is those six tokens, in order, at their true proportions. No new colour, no new component.
Two rules the bar needs. A segment under 2% still renders at 2% so it cannot vanish — a person with three dropped films out of four hundred should still see that they drop things. And the bar is never the only carrier: the legend below it names each state with its count, because six hues in a row is exactly the case the foundations warn about.

Standard deviation, in words. 16.7 on a 100-point scale means this person spreads their scores. Shown as a number it is trivia; shown as “you rate generously, and you rarely sit on the fence” it is the most personal line on the page. Two thresholds, three sentences, no new data.
What stays from the first draft. totals.partial still forces “at least 18.4 days” rather than an undercount. Mean score still reads — below three ratings. And there are still no streaks, no percentiles and no comparison — every number is cumulative, and skipping a month costs nothing. AL-chan is the reference for how a tracker should feel, not for turning a record into a scoreboard.

What the design needs to exist

Stated as requirements, not as a schema — how these are stored is the architecture agent's call. What matters to the design is that each of these can be read, written and ordered, and that two of them survive TMDB changing underneath us.

The design needsShapeNon-negotiable because
Display nameSeparate from the handleThe handle is unique and immutable-ish; the display name is neither. P7 truncates them differently.
BioFree text, 280 charactersThe editor draws a live counter against that number, and the card clamps at four lines.
Banner referenceOne value, plus which kind it is — catalogue still, colour, or uploaded fileE3 offers four sources and ticks the current one. Without the kind, the sheet cannot show what is already selected.
Avatar referenceOptional. Absent is a valid, designed state.The initial-on-a-gradient is the default, not a fallback — see §06.
Joined dateA dateRendered beside the handle on every profile, including P2's “joined today”.
Favourite peopleA person referenceNew — nothing stores this today.
Favourite charactersA TMDB credit_idOne value rehydrates the whole card via /credit/{id}. §01.
Order, on all four shelvesExplicit, person-setThe first film on a shelf is a statement. Films and series rely on user_library.is_favourite, which is a bare boolean today and has no order at all.
Copied display strings on every favouriteCharacter/person name, title, image path — stored, not joinedR-C3. TMDB credits are user-edited and can vanish. A profile must not degrade because a stranger tidied a cast list. This is the one place denormalisation is the requirement rather than the compromise.
Mean scoreAverage of ratings, with a countThe tile shows — below three ratings, so it needs the count as well as the average.
Two behaviours the interface cannot guarantee on its own. The eight-per-shelf cap — E7's “which one does this replace?” sheet is the designed path, but a cap enforced only in a sheet is not enforced. And the 280-character bio, for the same reason. Both are stated here so they are not read as presentation details.
Uploads need storage that does not exist yet — no bucket, no policies. That is a build question, not a design one; §06 recommends the catalogue-first route partly because it sidesteps it entirely, and the upload flow (E4, E5) is drawn either way.
10

Decisions

Everything above is designed and buildable. These four are yours.

#DecisionRecommendation
D1Followers and following. You asked for follow counts. On 2026-09-13 you parked Layers 3 and 4 — public profiles and follows — indefinitely, on the grounds that both need a community to be worth anything and there is no rollout planned. Six of the seven things on this page work perfectly in a private instrument; this one does not.Leave it parked. A follower count that reads 0 forever is worse than no tile, and the surface it opens — cross-user RLS, a public read policy, moderation — is the largest single piece of work in the product. The four-tile strip is designed without it and does not have a hole where it would go. Reverse it when there is a second person.
D2Upload, or catalogue only? Both are designed. This is a product call about what a banner is on a film tracker.Catalogue only, first. Faster to use, impossible to get wrong visually, and more personal on a tracker than a photograph. Upload is fully drawn; add it if you miss it.
D3Does this module replace you, or sit beside it? you already owns taste, stats and settings.Absorb it. One screen — identity and favourites on top, the existing taste cards below, settings behind a control in the header. Two screens showing the same numbers is how they drift apart, and there is no second user to need a separate public view.
D4How many per shelf? I recommended eight. The benchmark says otherwise: the account I looked at has roughly 25 favourite anime, shown as a full grid on its own tab. AL-chan does not cap.Both, and the tabs make it free. The Overview shelf shows the first 8 as a rail — that is the statement, and the constraint still does its work. The Favourites tab shows everything as a grid, uncapped. The cap becomes a display rule rather than a limit, which is what AL-chan is actually doing. I was wrong to make it a limit.
D5How long is a bio? I specified 280 characters. The account I pulled has a 10,104-character bio: markdown, images, custom headers, a Discord card, links to MAL and Steam. AL-chan profiles are a decorating culture.Longer, but not a canvas — 1,000 characters, plain text with links. 280 is a tweet and too short for “what I watch and why”. But the full markdown canvas brings an editor, image hosting, sanitising and a maintenance surface, for a product with one user. If you want the canvas, that is a real product direction and it should be its own decision rather than a field length.
Nothing here is built. This is design, for grooming. The acceptance criteria are yours to write, and the working order has not changed: concept → design → grooming → development.
