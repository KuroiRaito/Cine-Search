Title Module
Projector · module 4 · the biggest one

Title

A film and a series are not one screen with a few fields hidden. Nor are they two — the data says five shapes, and it says one shape people assume exists cannot be detected at all. Everything below was checked against live TMDB before it was drawn, including two things I got wrong on the first pass.

01

What the data says

A cohort is only real if the app can tell which one it is looking at. So the first question is not “what kinds of title are there” — it is what does TMDB let us distinguish. Sampled live:

SeriestypestatusSeasonsEpsnext_episode_to_airin_production
SeveranceScriptedReturning Series319—yes
Black MirrorScriptedReturning Series733—yes
Breaking BadScriptedEnded562—no
ChernobylMiniseriesEnded15—no
Three usable discriminators, and one surprise. type singles out a Miniseries. status separates returning from ended. in_production and next_episode_to_air together separate renewed and scheduled from renewed and not scheduled — and the surprise is that Severance is returning, in production, and has no next episode. “Coming back, nobody knows when” is a real and common state, and it is not the same screen as “next Friday”.
An anthology cannot be detected, so it cannot be a cohort. Black Mirror and True Detective carry an anthology keyword; Fargo does not — its keywords are detective, minnesota, winter, deputy, north dakota. Two out of three is not a discriminator, it is a coin toss with extra steps. So there is no anthology cohort, and the series design must instead degrade gracefully when seasons are unrelated: never imply that season 4 continues season 3, never say “continue watching” across a season boundary, and let the season picker carry its own year.

02

Variations and cohorts

Two levels, not one. The module holds three variations — three genuinely different pages — and each variation has cohorts inside it, which are the states that page can be in.

Title module — every page that describes something
 ├─ Movie page   TM   ├─ released
 │                      └─ upcoming
 ├─ Series page  TV   ├─ not yet premiered
 │                      ├─ running, scheduled
 │                      ├─ running, unscheduled
 │                      └─ finished
 └─ Person page  TP   ├─ director     progress
                        ├─ writer       progress
                        ├─ actor        roles, no progress
                        ├─ crew         camera, sound, editing
                        └─ plural       more than one of the above
The distinction is what each level may change.

A cohort may change the primary action, change what progress means, and omit bands. It may never add one, and never reorder them.
A variation may add and reorder bands — a person page has no providers and no episodes; a series page has an episode list a film never will.

Corrected after review: the first version said a cohort may change only the action and the progress, and my own TM2 broke it — an upcoming film drops providers, scores and synopsis. Omission is a cohort's right; addition is not.

Anthology is not a cohort of the series page, because it cannot be detected. Black Mirror and True Detective carry an anthology keyword; Fargo does not. Two of three is a coin toss with extra steps. So the series page instead never assumes continuity across a season boundary: no “continue” spanning seasons, and the season picker carries each season's own year. Nine cohorts, three variations, one spine. §03 is the band order every variation follows — a person page simply has fewer bands, in the same sequence.
03

The spine

The order every cohort follows. Sections that have nothing to say are absent, never empty — and the order never changes, so the page is the same place twice.

Band 2 is the entire cohort system. Five cohorts, five different buttons, one position. Somebody who arrives at a series from Search and a film from their library finds the thing they came to do in the same place on both, and never has to read the page to locate it.
03b

Seasons and episodes

Both looked like candidate variations. Neither is — but the reason each is not is different, and in both cases something real was being thrown away.

Seasons are tabs

A row of tabs across the top of the episode band — S1 S2 S3 — and the list below switches. The tab carries the season's identity, which is what a season page would have been for: its own name, its year, its count, and its progress against that season rather than the whole run.

Four things the row has to survive, all found by looking:

FoundWhat it forces
Grey's Anatomy has 24 seasonsThe row scrolls horizontally and the selected tab is scrolled into view on arrival — landing on season 21 of 24 with the row showing S1–S4 is the failure this prevents. Never a dropdown: a dropdown hides how long the show is, and how long it is happens to be the most useful fact on the page.
Season 0 exists, and holds 68 episodes on Grey'sSpecials get a tab, and it is last, never first — it is excluded from the denominator, so leading with it would put an unticked 68 in front of somebody's real progress. Labelled Specials, never Season 0.
Chernobyl's one season is called “Miniseries”Tabs use season.name, never a composed “Season N”. And with one season there is no tab row at all — a single tab is a label pretending to be a control.
Seasons exist with no poster and no air dateThe tab is a name and a count. Artwork is decoration here and its absence changes nothing.
Per-season progress is the thing the tabs unlock. “11 of 62” across five seasons tells you almost nothing; “S2 · 4 of 13” tells you where you are. Each tab carries its own fraction, and the whole-run number stays in the band above — both true, at the scale each is useful.
And it is addressable. ?season=2 deep-links with that tab selected, which is most of what a season page would have bought — for a query parameter rather than a route, a layout and a back-stack entry.
This is also what makes the undetectable anthology safe. A tab carrying its own name and year reads correctly whether or not it continues the last one, and per-season progress never implies a run. The graceful degradation §02 asks for arrives as a side effect rather than a special case.

An episode is a sheet, not a page

You never arrive at an episode cold. You are always inside a list of sixty-two, and a page would take you out of it — costing your scroll position to show you a still and a paragraph.

Gesture on an episode rowDoes
Tap the tickMarks it. The row's whole reason to exist, unchanged.
Tap the nameOpens the episode sheet — still, overview, air date, runtime, guest stars.
Anywhere elseNothing. Two targets, not one with a hotspot.
The sheet is where per-episode rating would go, and that is the point of choosing a sheet. We do not support it — rating is per title today — and it is a real product question rather than a layout one. A sheet leaves the room for it without committing to it, and without a route that would have to be un-built if the answer is no.
03d

Three rules the first drawings broke

All three came out of review, all three were repeated across several frames, and all three are cheap to state and easy to get wrong again.

A primary button is one line. Stacking a label over a caption — Continue over S2 E5 · Breakage — pushes the control to 52px and makes it read as two controls in a box. The verb takes the button; the detail goes beside it on the same line when it is short, and below the button when it is not, on the line that already states progress.

So: Continue · S2 E5 in the button, “11 of 62 · next up Breakage” underneath. And Caught up stands alone, because 19 of 19 aired was already written directly below it — the button was repeating the page.
Season selectors are pills. One line, --radius-full, the count inline beside the number rather than stacked under it. A two-line tab in a scrolling row reads as a stack of small boxes, which is what made the first version off-putting.
A rating is a filled star and a number, side by side. Always. Never stacked, never outlined. An outlined star is the empty half of a rating control — using it to display a score says the opposite of what is meant, and it is the difference between “rated 9” and “rate this”.

Lucide's star is a stroke icon by default, so the rating star is drawn with fill="currentColor" and a lighter stroke. This applies everywhere a score appears — tile badges, episode rows, the scores band, the library's compact row.

The first draft drew a row of logos, which is a list rather than a design. TMDB separates the buckets, and the separation answers a question the logos do not: what will this cost me?

My second draft had three tiers and the first one was fiction. I designed “On your services” against profiles.services — and that column is read by AuthProvider and settable nowhere. There is no settings screen for it, so it is '{}' for everybody, and the tier would have been permanently empty. Exactly the failure I caught in the library's recommended_by, made by me this time. Two tiers.
TierBucketsDrawn as
Streamingflatrate + free + adsOne row of logos with names. ads carries a quiet with ads; free carries free. Everything here is watchable without paying per title.
Rent or buyrent + buyOne chip: Rent or buy · 5, which opens a sheet. Seven storefronts for Oppenheimer in the US, and the same seven for nearly everything — a list adds nothing a count does not.
Two rows, not six — and that was the other complaint. The three-tier version pushed the scores, your rating and the synopsis below the fold on a 320px screen, which is the richest data on the page losing its place to a list of shops. Streaming is one row; rent is one chip. Everything above the fold stays above it.
Deduplicated upward. Apple TV Store appears in both rent and buy for Oppenheimer; a provider already shown as streaming is never repeated below, and one offering both rent and buy is counted once.
If services ever becomes settable — a settings screen listing the services somebody subscribes to — the streaming tier splits in two and yours come first. The design is ready for it. It is not built on it.
No prices, ever. TMDB does not carry them. No “from £3.49”, no cheapest-first. Inventing a price on a page somebody acts on is the one failure here that costs them money.
04

Wikipedia

You do not need to find a way to link them. TMDB already hands us the key. external_ids returns a wikidata_id for films, series and people, and Wikidata resolves it to every Wikipedia article about that thing, in every language. The whole chain is two public calls and no key.
TMDB   /movie/693134/external_ids        → wikidata_id: Q109228991
Wikidata  wbgetentities?ids=Q109228991   → sitelinks in 54 languages, enwiki: "Dune: Part Two"
Wikipedia /page/summary/Dune:_Part_Two   → description, extract, canonical URL

Verified end to end for all three entity types. What comes back is more useful than a link:

FieldDune: Part TwoWorth
description“2024 film by Denis Villeneuve”The prize. One human-written line that says what a thing is. TMDB has no equivalent — it has genres and a marketing synopsis.
extractThe lead paragraph, encyclopedic in toneA second voice for the overview. Reads completely unlike TMDB's copy.
content_urlsThe canonical article URLThe link, correctly escaped.
sitelinks54 languages (film) · 32 (series) · 61 (person)Localisation for free, when it is wanted.

Coverage, measured — and it decides the design

A Wikipedia article is absent more often than it is present — for series, two times in three. So this is an enhancement and never a dependency. Every band in §03 must be complete without it, the block simply is not drawn when there is nothing, and nothing anywhere says “no Wikipedia article”. A hole where an enhancement would have been is worse than no enhancement.

How to show it

WhereWhatWhy there
Under the titleThe description line — 2024 film by Denis VilleneuveThe single best thing here. It answers “what is this” before any metadata line does, in words a person wrote. One line, --text-body-sm, muted.
In the overview bandA Background block holding the extract, under TMDB's synopsis, attributedTwo voices, not one merged one. TMDB sells the film; Wikipedia describes it. Somebody who has read the synopsis and still does not know what the thing is gets an answer.
End of that block“Read on Wikipedia” with the canonical URLAttribution and the exit, in one control.
Nowhere else—No badge, no tab, no second poster, no infobox scraping.
Never the plot section. Only the lead extract. /page/summary/ returns the article's lead paragraph, which is safe by convention. The article body contains a full plot summary with every twist in it, and a film tracker that spoils the film is worse than one with no background at all. If the extract ever needs extending, extend it by more of the lead and never by another section.
Three more constraints the source brings with it.
Licence. Wikipedia text is CC BY-SA. Quoting the extract requires visible attribution and a link — which the design has anyway, but it is now a requirement rather than a courtesy.
It is user-edited. Same lesson as the character favourites: an article can be wrong, stale, or vandalised for an hour. It is labelled as Wikipedia's words, never presented as ours.
It is a third-party request. The page must render before it arrives and reflow without jumping — so the block appears below the fold, and its absence is indistinguishable from its never having been attempted.
One thing it quietly fixes. TMDB overviews are sometimes empty, and on an obscure or foreign-language title they often are. Today that band is just missing. With this, a title with no TMDB synopsis but a Wikipedia article now has something to say — and the measured numbers suggest that is exactly where the two sources least overlap.
05

Variation 3 · the person page

The third variation, and the one that is least like the other two. It shares the spine and drops half of it — no providers, no episodes, no status, no rating. What it has instead is a body of work, and the question that comes with one.

A person page is a description page, so it belongs in this module. Settled 2026-09-17. It describes a thing using a different kind of data — the same relationship a series page has to a film page. Its cohorts are director, writer, actor, crew and plural.

For the development side, not this document: src/modules/person/ is a separate module today. The design says one module with three variations; the code says two. One of them should move, and that is a build decision.
A title page asksA person page asks
The questionShould I watch this?What of theirs have I not seen?
The verbWatch, track, rateExplore, and complete
Progress meansEpisodes tickedTitles seen out of a body of work
The payoffWhere to watch it tonight“6 of 26 directed”

Two experiments, both failed, and the answer is in the failures

The question is not academic: how do you tell somebody who does not already know that Villeneuve is a director and Cillian Murphy is an actor? Three signals exist. I tested all three.

SignalResultVerdict
known_for_departmentGerwig → Acting, despite Director × 6 including Barbie and Lady BirdNot sufficient. Right often, badly wrong sometimes.
Popularity of creditsEvery single person came out an actor — because their most popular credit is The Tonight Show, The Late Show, Watch What Happens Live. A one-off guest slot on a two-thousand-episode series outranks a career.Broken, until talk, news and reality genres are excluded. Then: Villeneuve → Directing, Murphy → Acting, both correct.
TMDB's own known_forGerwig → Frances Ha, No Strings Attached, Jackie. All acting roles. Neither Barbie nor Lady Bird.Stale. It is popularity-shaped and lags a career turning.
So the page never labels the person. It shows the evidence, and lets the reader do what the data cannot.

Under the name, a line of verbs and titles rather than a job title:

Directed Barbie, Lady Bird · Acted in Frances Ha, Jackie

Somebody who has never heard of Greta Gerwig learns more from “Directed Barbie” than from the word Director — and it is the one formulation that cannot be wrong, because it is not a claim, it is a list.

Three sources, each doing the one thing it is good at

SourceDoesExample
Wikipedia descriptionThe label — because a human wrote it, and no computed one beats that“American filmmaker and actress” · “Canadian filmmaker (born 1967)”
The creditsThe evidence — verbs and recognisable titlesDirected Barbie, Lady Bird
Filtered popularityThe order of the sections belowDirecting above Acting, or neither above the other
This is what the Wikipedia work in §04 was actually for. “American filmmaker and actress” answers in five words what two algorithms could not, and it is available for people at the same rate as for films. When it is missing the evidence line stands alone — which still works, because the evidence line was never leaning on it.
When the top two are close, neither wins — and that is a result, not a fallback. Gerwig comes out 64 acting against 58 directing with talk shows excluded. Inside 25% the page shows both sections at equal weight, in that order, with no “primarily an actor” anywhere. She is both, the numbers say so, and a page that picked one would be asserting something the data explicitly refuses to.
The talk-show exclusion is a rule, not a tweak. Genres 10767 (Talk), 10763 (News) and 10764 (Reality) are removed from the ranking signal — never from the filmography, where they are real credits somebody earned. They are excluded from deciding what a person is, because appearing on Fallon is not a body of work.
Name collisions are real and were found by making one. Searching Roger Deakins returns two people: the cinematographer (151, Camera, Director of Photography × 74) and a makeup artist (4313). A person page must therefore always carry enough identity — department, era, a known title — that arriving at the wrong one is obvious immediately. State TP5.

Two real people, found by fetching the wrong one. A person row always carries department, top job and a known title — enough that arriving at the wrong Roger Deakins is obvious in under a second.

The rule the person page runs on: name the work, never the person. No job title anywhere — not a badge, not a subtitle. The line under the name is verbs and titles, Directed Barbie · Acted in Frances Ha, because that cannot be wrong: it is a list, not a claim. Wikipedia's one-liner sits above it when there is one, since a human wrote that and no computed label beats it.

Sections below are ordered by filtered popularity, and a progress bar appears only on Director and Writer. “6 of 26 directed” is a canon somebody works through; “6 of 202 scored” is not the same claim, and a bar would be inventing an ambition nobody has.
The filters are what make a person page useful to somebody tracking. Not seen is the one that earns its place — “show me the twenty Villeneuve films I have not watched” is the actual task, and it is the only question this page can answer that no other page can. The role filters (Directed, Wrote, Acted) come from the credit counts, so a person with one body of work gets one chip and no row.

Decisions

#DecisionRecommendation
TD1Does Wikipedia ship? It is two public calls, no key, and gives a one-line description, a lead extract and a canonical URL.Yes — the description line first, the Background block second. The line under the title is the highest value per pixel in this document and works for films, series and people. The Background block is worth having but is absent for two series in three, so ship the line alone if only one lands.
TD2Is “caught up” a status, or a state? TV2 shows it as a computed state, not one of the six.A state. The six statuses are what a person declares; caught-up is arithmetic the app does. Making it a seventh status would mean it going stale the moment an episode airs — and somebody would have to un-declare it.
TD3Percentages. Shown on finished series only, in this design.Keep it that way. On a running series the denominator moves, so a percentage is a number that goes down when a new episode airs. That is the kind of detail people notice once and never trust again.
TD5Season and episode — variations, or not?Settled: neither. A season is a scope — the episode band takes its identity, addressable as ?season=2, which is most of what a page buys for a query parameter instead of a route. An episode is a sheet, because you never arrive at one cold and a page would cost you your place in a list of sixty-two. The sheet is also where per-episode rating would land if it is ever wanted, without committing to it now.
TD6The studios shelf on the profile lets somebody favourite a thing the product cannot show them.Settled: dropped, rather than adding a studio variation to justify it. It was the emptiest shelf in the benchmark — 0 to 11 entries against characters at 25. Removed from profile-module.html in the same change.
TD4Prefix collision. This module wants T; the profile document already uses T1–T2 for its stats tab.Rename the profile's two to ST1–ST2, which is a two-line edit in a document nobody has written tests against yet — cheaper than TT on the largest module in the product.
Nothing here is built. Design only — §01 is a reading of the live API, not of the code.
